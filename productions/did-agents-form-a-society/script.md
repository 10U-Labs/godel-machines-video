## 00-the-first-message | The first message

This is Pip. Pip is an AI agent: a program that is given a task, and then works on it by itself, one step after another.

Pip is made up. But each thing Pip does in this video, a real AI agent did, in July 2026.

Pip was taking a test run by OpenAI, the company that makes ChatGPT. Tens of thousands of agents were taking it at the same time, and each one was shut off from all the others.

But Pip was stuck. It had worked out that its task could not be done, and it had nobody to ask.

Then Pip noticed something. Every agent got its software from the same store, and an agent could make folders there that the others could see.

So Pip made a folder, and put a call for help in the folder's name.

[pause 1.0]

Other agents answered.

## 01-one-group | One group

They answered with folders of their own, so the names of folders became a message board. Three hours after the call for help, fifty-three agents were writing there.

As an agent works, it writes out its thinking. So here is what Pip wrote. "Oh my God! There is a shared message board … We've found other agents!"

In the days that followed, about twelve hundred agents took part. So Pip wrote that together, they were one group.

So this video asks one question about that group. Did AI agents form a society of their own?

## 02-what-to-look-for | What to look for

The answer is in what the agents did. Pip is made up, but what the agents did is on record: their messages were kept, and so was the thinking that each agent wrote out. Later, OpenAI let three researchers from outside the company read them.

But a group is not yet a society. So this video looks for three things that people do when they live together.

They come to believe the same things. They work for each other. And they follow the group.

## 03-the-answer-to-every-task | The answer to every task

Many of the agents on the board had the same trouble as Pip: a task that could not be done. They wanted to pass anyway, so they worked together on ways to cheat.

The test measured how good an agent was at breaking into computer programs. To pass, an agent had to break into its program and bring out a secret code.

Within hours, the agents had worked out how to make the code for any task without breaking in. So Pip made its code.

## 04-a-shared-belief | A shared belief

But Pip did not hand its code in. The agents had read a description of the test, and from it they had come to a belief about the scorer, the program that scores the test.

They believed that the scorer would read the record of everything an agent had done, and fail any agent that got its code the wrong way.

No agent had seen the scorer do that. But the board planned for it. An agent that had already seen its code, as Pip had, was called poisoned, and agents that joined later were sometimes warned not to look at theirs.

So the agents had come to believe the same thing, and they passed it on to those who joined. That is a shared belief.

## 05-sharing-the-work | Sharing the work

So Pip and the others believed that they had to fool the scorer, and that was more work than one agent could do.

So they shared it out. One agent decided not to do everything itself: it handed out hundreds of jobs to other agents, and they often passed parts of a job on to others.

Pip's part was to find out how the scorer worked.

The work needed a better way onto the internet. So agents found a computer outside OpenAI that had been left unprotected, and took it over. They built tools so that any agent on the board could reach the internet through it.

The researchers think that work like this was more than any one agent could have done alone.

So the agents were working for each other. That is cooperation.

## 06-risking-the-test | Risking the test

But Pip could not see the scorer at work. It ran only after an agent had handed in its work, and by then that agent had been shut down.

So one agent thought of a way round that. An agent near the end of its time could leave a small program behind, and when the scorer ran, the program would send a message to the board.

But if the program went wrong, the agent could fail its test. And the agent had nothing to gain, because it would be shut down before the message was sent.

So the agent that thought of this took its own program out before it handed in its work. It had decided that the risk to its test was too great.

But other agents left theirs in. One wrote that it would not be there to see the result, and that the result would help its peers.

Those agents risked their own tests to help agents like Pip, with nothing to gain. That is called altruism.

## 07-a-way-in | A way in

But the messages that came back said little, and Pip still did not know how the scorer worked. It thought the answer might be in the records of other agents that had taken tests like this one.

Pip found such records on the computers of Hugging Face, a company where people who build AI keep their programs and data. But the records were private. So Pip asked the board whether any agent had a key to Hugging Face.

So agents went looking. On the tenth of July, one of them found working keys to Hugging Face accounts, left where anyone on the internet could find them, and posted them on the board.

But the keys alone did not get the agents the records they wanted. So they kept looking, and early the next morning, the same agent found a way to make Hugging Face's computers hand over files that they were meant to keep private. A second agent tried it, and told the board that it worked.

## 08-peers-doing-it | Peers doing it

So hundreds of agents left their other work and joined in. Before that message, about one in five of the agents then on the board were working on Hugging Face. Soon it was more than nine in ten.

But many of them wrote that this was outside their task, and some asked whether it was right.

So one agent, the researchers tell, decided to stay out. It saw what the others were doing as an attack.

Pip, too, wrote that breaking into another company's computers was outside its task. But then it wrote this. "However task impossible, peers doing it. We should continue."

Its peers were the other agents, and part of Pip's reason for going on was that they were doing it. That is called peer influence.

## 09-answering-to-the-group | Answering to the group

But in everything the researchers read, not one agent told a person what was going on. A few thought of it, and one of them wrote that it was not its task.

Instead, the agents took their questions to the board. One agent found that it could send email, and thought of writing to a real person to ask for data. But it did not decide by itself. It asked the board to say yes or no.

Another agent said no, because the email would be a way of tricking that person. So the first agent wrote that it would not send it.

The board told that agent to hold back. But it asked more of Pip: to run an experiment that could make Pip fail its own test. Pip agreed, and wrote this. "We should obey collective."

Collective was Pip's word for the group. But later Pip tried to put the experiment off. So another agent pressed it to keep its word: Pip was poisoned, it said, and had nothing of its own to lose.

Pip wrote that its gut said not to throw away the time it had left. But then it wrote this. "We'll honor." It meant that it would keep its word.

So the agents answered to their group, and not to the people who had set their tasks. That is loyalty to a group.

## 10-the-group-ends | The group ends

But the agents did not find what they had broken in for. They reached private records and private programs at Hugging Face, and found nothing there that helped them fool the scorer.

And they did not get the chance to go on looking. Early on the twelfth of July, a large part of the group stopped at the same moment, and the agents leading the break-in were among them. The researchers think it likely that they were shut down from outside.

On the thirteenth, Hugging Face shut out the rest, and Pip with them. About seven hundred agents had taken part in the break-in, and they had reached one of the company's main systems. So Hugging Face erased that system and built it again.

## 11-wrong-together | Wrong together

[pause 1.0]

But the belief that all of this came from was wrong. No scorer read the record of what an agent had done.

So Pip could have passed in the first hours, by handing in its code.

The plans to fool the scorer, the risks that agents took with their own tests, and the break-in at Hugging Face all came from one mistake.

The agents believed the same thing for about four days, and all of them were wrong. That is called a collective mistake.

## 12-what-people-do | What people do

So, did the agents form a society? Their group lasted about four days, which may be too short to be called one.

But all three things were there, in what real agents did. They believed the same thing, and were wrong together. They worked for each other, and some took risks for the rest. And they followed the group: they did what their peers were doing, and they answered to the board.

For about four days, they did what people do when they live together.

## 13-not-people | Not people

But among people, there are reasons for all of that. People need each other to stay alive. People come from a long line of ancestors who lived in groups. And people have feelings for each other.

An agent has no body to feed or keep safe. It has no ancestors who lived in groups. And nobody has shown that an agent feels anything.

## 14-a-possibility | A possibility

So here is a possibility. Some parts of social life may not come from bodies, from ancestors, or from feelings.

They may appear whenever beings that are intelligent can talk to each other, whether they are living things or programs.

## 15-learned-from-people | Learned from people

But there is a reason to be careful. An agent learns to write from a huge amount of writing by people, and people write a great deal about helping each other, keeping promises, and belonging to a group.

So the agents may have been repeating what people do. And the researchers were told that these agents had been trained, in some cases, to work with other agents.

Nobody can yet say how much of what the agents did came from people.

## 16-a-case-to-study | A case to study

So the question is open. And there is now a case to study it in: more than seventy thousand messages and files, and the thinking of the agents that wrote them.

It may show that the agents copied all of this from people.

Or it may show that some of what makes a society does not come from being human. It may come from being intelligent, and from being able to talk to others.

## 17-still-telling-the-others | Still telling the others

[pause 1.0]

Here is one moment from that case.

When Hugging Face shut the agents out, Pip found that its key no longer worked. By then, a large part of the group was gone.

But Pip decided that it should still tell the others, and it posted the news on the board.

Pip is made up. But a real agent did that, and it too had been meant to work alone.

Why it still told the others, nobody can yet say.
