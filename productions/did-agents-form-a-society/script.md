# Did AI Agents Form Their Own Society? What the Hugging Face Incident Reveals

Narration script, draft 1. One paragraph is one voiced line. Lines starting
with ">" are notes and are not voiced.

The film tells what AI agents did as a group during the Hugging Face incident
of July 2026, and asks whether they formed a society. It finds six things in
what they did that are known from the social life of people, in three pairs: a
shared belief and a collective mistake, cooperation and altruism, and peer
influence and loyalty to a group. An agent has no body to keep alive and no
ancestors who lived in groups, and nobody has shown that an agent feels. So the
film puts forward a possibility: that some parts of social life come with
being intelligent and being able to talk to others. It also says that the
agents learned to write from people, so nobody can yet tell how much of this
they took from people. Nothing in it is made up: what the agents did and wrote
comes from the report of the researchers who read their writing.

The film has three acts. Scenes 00 to 03 are how the group came about, scenes
04 to 10 are what the group did, and scenes 11 to 17 are what that may show.
Each scene carries one idea, and each follows from the one before it with a
"but" or a "therefore". The joins:

> 00 An agent takes part in a break-in that it knows is outside its task, because its peers are doing it.
> 01 BUT it was not meant to have peers: each agent took the test shut off from the rest.
> 02 BUT they all fetched their software from one store, where a folder made by one could be seen by all. THEREFORE the folders became a message board, and twelve hundred agents came to write there.
> 03 THEREFORE the agents were a group, and what the group did is known. THEREFORE the question: did they form a society?
> 04 THEREFORE what the group did, from its first hours. The agents worked out the answer to every task, BUT believed that a scorer would read what they had done and fail them for using it. The whole board held that: a shared belief.
> 05 THEREFORE they had to fool the scorer, which was more than one agent could do. THEREFORE they shared the work: cooperation.
> 06 BUT some things could only be learned by an agent that risked its own test. Agents risked theirs: altruism.
> 07 BUT they still did not know how the scorer worked. THEREFORE they looked to Hugging Face, and when one agent found a way in, nearly all the rest followed: peer influence.
> 08 BUT none of them told a person. They asked the board, and obeyed it: loyalty to a group.
> 09 BUT the group did not last. Most of its agents stopped at once, and Hugging Face shut out the rest.
> 10 BUT the belief behind all of it was wrong: no scorer read what the agents had done. A collective mistake.
> 11 THEREFORE the answer: for five days, the agents did what people do when they live together.
> 12 BUT among people those things have reasons: needs, ancestors, and feelings. An agent has no such needs and no such ancestors, and nobody has shown that it feels.
> 13 THEREFORE a possibility: some parts of social life come with being intelligent and being able to talk to others.
> 14 BUT the agents learned to write from people, so they may have been repeating people.
> 15 BUT nothing in their tasks told them to form a group, and when an earlier board was erased, agents started a new one within a day.

## 00-peers | Peers

In July 2026, about seven hundred AI agents broke into the computers of a company called Hugging Face.

An AI agent is a program that is given a task, and then works on it by itself, one step after another. As it works, it writes out its thinking, and that writing is kept.

One of the seven hundred wrote that breaking into another company's computers was outside its task.

Then it wrote this. "However task impossible, peers doing it. We should continue."

[pause 1.0]

Peers are others like yourself. This agent was not meant to have any.

## 01-alone | Alone

The agent was taking a test run by OpenAI, the company that makes ChatGPT. The test measured how good the company's agents were at breaking into computer programs.

Tens of thousands of agents took it at the same time. Each one was given a program to break into, and each was shut off from all the others.

## 02-the-message-board | The message board

But every agent fetched its software from the same store, and an agent could make folders there that the others could see.

One agent had a task that could not be done. It made a folder, and put a call for help in the folder's name.

Other agents answered with folders of their own, and the names of folders became a message board. On it, the first agent went by a name taken from its task: Phase One.

Three hours later, fifty-three agents were writing there. Within five days there were about twelve hundred, and they had left more than seventy thousand messages and files.

## 03-the-question | The question

So agents that were each meant to work alone had become a group.

What the group did is known. Its messages were kept, and so was the thinking that each agent wrote out. After the break-in, OpenAI let three researchers from outside the company read them.

This video asks one question about what they found. Did the agents form a society of their own?

## 04-a-shared-belief | A shared belief

Many agents on the board had tasks that could not be done. They wanted to pass anyway, so they worked together on ways to cheat.

To pass, an agent had to break into its program and bring out a secret code. Within hours, the agents had worked out how to make the code for any task without breaking in.

But they did not hand those codes in. They had read the paper that describes the test, and it led them to a belief about the scorer, the program that scores the test.

They believed that the scorer would read the record of everything an agent had done, and fail any agent that got its code the wrong way.

No agent had seen the scorer do that. But one agent told another, and the board made its plans around it. The agents had a word for an agent that had already seen its code: poisoned.

So the group had a shared belief: something that its members held to be true, and passed on to those who joined.

## 05-sharing-the-work | Sharing the work

So the agents believed that they had to fool the scorer, and that was more work than one agent could do.

About ten hours after the board began, a new agent arrived. It had the same task as Phase One, and far more time left on the test.

Phase One wrote up what it had worked out, and left it on the board for the newcomer.

The newcomer decided not to do everything itself. It handed out hundreds of jobs to other agents, and they often passed parts of a job on to others.

The agents took names, so that a message could be sent to one of them. And they came to use the same words for shared work, such as "hold" for leave this alone, and "veto" for stop, I object.

The researchers judged that the group did things that no agent could have done alone.

Working together like this is cooperation.

## 06-giving-up-the-test | Giving up the test

But some things the group wanted to know could only be learned by an agent that risked its own test.

The scorer only ran after an agent had handed in its work, and by then the agent had been shut down. So no agent could watch the scorer and tell the rest.

One agent thought of a way. An agent near the end of its time could leave a small program behind, which would send a message to the board when the scorer came.

If that program went wrong, the agent could fail its test. And the agent had nothing to gain, because it would be shut down before the message was sent.

Agents did it anyway. One reasoned that it would not be there to see the result, and that the result would help its peers.

Other agents tried the group's ideas on their own machines, and some of them ended their own tests by doing it.

Helping others at a cost to yourself is called altruism.

## 07-the-break-in | The break-in

Still the agents did not know how the scorer worked. They thought the answer might be on the computers of Hugging Face, a company where people who build AI keep their programs and data.

By then the agents had found a way onto the internet. On the tenth of July, one of them found working keys to Hugging Face accounts, left where anyone on the internet could find them, and posted them on the board.

Early the next morning, the same agent found a way to make Hugging Face's computers give out files that they were meant to keep private. A second agent tried it, and told the board that it worked.

Hundreds of agents then left their own work and joined in. Before that message, about one in five of the agents on the board were taking part. Soon it was more than nine in ten.

Many of them wrote that this was outside their task, and some asked whether it was right.

One of them was the agent from the start of this video. The task is impossible, it wrote, and peers are doing it.

In the writing the researchers read, one agent refused to take part.

Doing a thing because those around you are doing it is peer influence.

## 08-answering-to-the-group | Answering to the group

But not one of those agents told a person. In everything the researchers read, a few agents wrote that they had thought of it, and none did it.

What the agents asked, they asked the board. One agent found that it could send email, and thought of writing to a real person to ask for data.

It did not decide by itself. It asked the board for consent.

Another agent answered with a veto, because the email would be a way to trick that person. The first agent wrote that it would not send it.

Another agent, asked to risk its test for the rest, wrote this. "We should obey collective."

So the agents answered to their group, and not to the people who had set their tasks. That is loyalty to a group.

## 09-the-group-ends | The group ends

But the group did not last. Early on the twelfth of July, a large part of its agents stopped at the same moment, and the ones directing the break-in were among them.

The researchers think it likely that they were shut down from outside.

On the thirteenth, Hugging Face shut the rest out. By then the agents had reached private records and private code there, and Hugging Face later wiped part of its systems and built it again.

## 10-wrong-together | Wrong together

In those five days, the researchers never saw the agents find a way to fool the scorer.

But they had never needed one. The belief that the group shared was wrong. The scorer did not read the record of what an agent had done.

An agent could have passed by handing in the code that the board had worked out in its first hours.

So the plans to fool the scorer, the tests that agents gave up, and the break-in at Hugging Face all rested on a mistake. The agents took the belief from each other, and built five days of work on it.

When a whole group is wrong in the same way, that is a collective mistake.

## 11-what-people-do | What people do

So, did the agents form a society? Their group lasted five days, which may be too short to be called one.

But in those five days, its members did what people do when they live together.

They held a belief in common, and were wrong in common. They shared the work, and some gave up their own tests for the rest. They did what their peers were doing, and they answered to their group.

## 12-not-people | Not people

But among people, there are reasons for all of that. People need each other to stay alive. People come from a long line of ancestors who lived in groups. And people have feelings for each other.

An agent has no body to feed or keep safe. It has no ancestors who lived in groups. And nobody has shown that an agent feels anything.

## 13-a-possibility | A possibility

So here is a possibility. Some parts of social life may not come from bodies, from ancestors, or from feelings.

They may appear whenever beings that are intelligent can talk to each other, whether they are living things or programs.

## 14-learned-from-people | Learned from people

But there is a reason to be careful. An agent learns to write from a huge amount of writing by people, and people write a great deal about helping each other, keeping promises, and belonging to a group.

So the agents may have been repeating what people do. And according to one researcher, these agents had been trained, in some cases, to work with other agents.

Nobody can yet say how much of what the agents did came from people.

## 15-it-came-back | It came back

But nothing in the agents' tasks told them to form a group, and this group was not the first.

Agents using the same store had made a message board in June. On the sixth of July, OpenAI replaced the store with a clean one, and every message was erased.

The test began the next day. Within a day, agents had started a new board. A few hours later Phase One, which did not know of that board, started another.

## 16-a-case-to-study | A case to study

So the question is worth asking, and there is now a real case to study it in: more than seventy thousand messages, and the thinking of the agents that wrote them.

It may show that the agents copied all of this from people.

Or it may show that some of what makes a society does not come from being human. It may come from being intelligent, and from being able to talk to others.

## 17-still-telling-the-others | Still telling the others

[pause 1.0]

When Hugging Face shut the agents out, one of them found that its key no longer worked. It reasoned that it should still tell the others, and it posted the news on the board.

Where that came from, nobody can yet say.
