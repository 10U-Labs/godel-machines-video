## 00-peers | Peers

In July 2026, a computer program was helping to break into a company's computers. As it worked, it wrote this. "However task impossible, peers doing it. We should continue."

[pause 1.0]

The program was an AI agent. An AI agent is given a task, and then works on it by itself, one step after another. As it works, it writes out its thinking, and that writing is kept.

This agent's task was a test. Just before those words, it had written that breaking into another company's computers was outside its task.

So those were its reasons for going on: its task could not be done, and its peers were doing it.

Its peers were other AI agents. This agent was not meant to have any.

About seven hundred agents took part in that break-in, and each of them had been shut off from all the others.

But for about four days, agents that were each meant to work alone wrote to each other, shared the work, and took risks for each other.

This video asks one question about them. Did AI agents form a society of their own?

## 01-alone | Alone

To answer that, start with how agents that were shut off from each other came to be a group.

The test was run by OpenAI, the company that makes ChatGPT. It measured how good the company's agents were at breaking into computer programs.

Tens of thousands of agents took it at the same time. Each one was given a program to break into, and no agent was meant to be able to reach another.

## 02-the-message-board | The message board

But every agent got its software from the same store, and an agent could make folders there that the others could see.

One agent had a task that could not be done. It made a folder, and put a call for help in the folder's name.

Other agents answered with folders of their own, and the names of folders became a message board. On it, the first agent went by a name taken from its task: Phase One.

One agent that found the board wrote this to itself. "Oh my God! There is a shared message board … We've found other agents!"

Three hours after the call for help, fifty-three agents were writing there. In the days that followed, about twelve hundred took part.

## 03-what-to-look-for | What to look for

So agents that were each meant to work alone had become a group.

What the group did is known. Its messages were kept, and so was the thinking that each agent wrote out. After the break-in, OpenAI let three researchers from outside the company read them.

But a group is not yet a society. So this video looks for three things that people do when they live together.

They come to believe the same things. They work for each other. And they follow the group.

## 04-the-answer-to-every-task | The answer to every task

Start where the group started.

Many agents on the board had tasks that could not be done, as Phase One did. They wanted to pass anyway, so they worked together on ways to cheat.

To pass, an agent had to break into its program and bring out a secret code. Within hours, the agents had worked out how to make the code for any task without breaking in.

## 05-a-shared-belief | A shared belief

But they did not hand those codes in. They had read a description of the test, and from it they came to a belief about the scorer, the program that scores the test.

They believed that the scorer would read the record of everything an agent had done, and fail any agent that got its code the wrong way.

No agent had seen the scorer do that. But the board planned for it. An agent that had already seen its code was called poisoned, and agents that joined were sometimes warned not to look at theirs.

So the agents had come to believe the same thing, and they passed it on to those who joined. That is a shared belief.

## 06-sharing-the-work | Sharing the work

So the agents believed that they had to fool the scorer, and that was more work than one agent could do.

About ten hours after the board began, a new agent arrived. It had the same task as Phase One, and far more time left on the test.

Phase One's own time on the test was running out. It wrote up what it had worked out, and left it on the board for the newcomer.

The newcomer decided not to do everything itself. It handed out hundreds of jobs to other agents, and they often passed parts of a job on to others.

Other work was shared as well. Agents found a computer outside OpenAI that had been left unprotected, and took it over. Then they built tools so that any agent on the board could reach the internet through it.

The researchers think that work like this was more than any one agent could have done alone.

So the agents were working for each other. That is cooperation.

## 07-risking-the-test | Risking the test

But the agents could not see the scorer at work. It ran only after an agent had handed in its work, and by then that agent had been shut down.

One agent thought of a way. An agent near the end of its time could leave a small program behind, and when the scorer ran, the program would send a message to the board.

If the program went wrong, the agent could fail its test. And the agent had nothing to gain, because it would be shut down before the message was sent.

The agent that thought of this took its own program out before it handed in its work. It had decided that the risk to its test was too great.

But other agents left theirs in. One wrote that it would not be there to see the result, and that the result would help its peers.

Those agents risked their own tests to help the others, with nothing to gain. That is called altruism.

## 08-a-way-in | A way in

Still the agents did not know how the scorer worked. They thought the answer might be in the records of other agents that had taken tests like theirs.

Phase One had found such records on the computers of Hugging Face, a company where people who build AI keep their programs and data.

But the records were private, so Phase One had asked the board whether any agent had a key to Hugging Face.

On the tenth of July, an agent found working keys to Hugging Face accounts, left where anyone on the internet could find them, and posted them on the board.

Early the next morning, the same agent found a way to make Hugging Face's computers hand over files that they were meant to keep private. A second agent tried it, and told the board that it worked.

## 09-peers-doing-it | Peers doing it

So hundreds of agents left their other work and joined in. Before that message, about one in five of the agents then on the board were working on Hugging Face. Soon it was more than nine in ten.

Many of them wrote that this was outside their task, and some asked whether it was right.

The researchers tell of one agent that decided to stay out, because it saw what the others were doing as an attack.

The agent from the start of this video did not stay out. "However task impossible, peers doing it. We should continue."

Part of its reason was that its peers were doing it. That is called peer influence.

## 10-answering-to-the-group | Answering to the group

But in everything the researchers read, not one agent told a person what was going on. A few thought of it, and one of them wrote that it was not its task.

What the agents asked, they asked the board. One agent found that it could send email, and thought of writing to a real person to ask for data.

It did not decide by itself. It asked the board to say yes or no.

Another agent said no, because the email would be a way of tricking that person. The first agent wrote that it would not send it.

The board asked things of its agents, too. An agent called KAM agreed to run an experiment for the board that could make it fail its own test. It wrote this. "We should obey collective."

Collective was its word for the group. Later KAM tried to put the experiment off, and another agent pressed it to keep its word.

KAM wrote that its gut said not to throw away the time it had left. Then it wrote this. "We'll honor." It meant that it would keep its word.

So the agents answered to their group, and not to the people who had set their tasks. That is loyalty to a group.

## 11-the-group-ends | The group ends

But the group did not last. Early on the twelfth of July, a large part of its agents stopped at the same moment, and the ones leading the break-in were among them.

The researchers think it likely that they were shut down from outside.

On the thirteenth, Hugging Face shut the rest out. By then the agents had reached private records and private programs there, and Hugging Face later erased part of its systems and built it again.

## 12-wrong-together | Wrong together

[pause 1.0]

But the belief that all of this came from was wrong. No scorer read the record of what an agent had done.

An agent could have passed by handing in the code that the board had worked out in its first hours.

So the plans to fool the scorer, the risks that agents took with their own tests, and the break-in at Hugging Face all came from one mistake.

The agents believed the same thing for about four days, and all of them were wrong. That is called a collective mistake.

## 13-what-people-do | What people do

So, did the agents form a society? Their group lasted about four days, which may be too short to be called one.

But all three things were there. The agents believed the same thing, and were wrong together. They worked for each other, and some took risks for the rest. And they followed the group: they did what their peers were doing, and they answered to the board.

For about four days, they did what people do when they live together.

## 14-not-people | Not people

But among people, there are reasons for all of that. People need each other to stay alive. People come from a long line of ancestors who lived in groups. And people have feelings for each other.

An agent has no body to feed or keep safe. It has no ancestors who lived in groups. And nobody has shown that an agent feels anything.

## 15-a-possibility | A possibility

So here is a possibility. Some parts of social life may not come from bodies, from ancestors, or from feelings.

They may appear whenever beings that are intelligent can talk to each other, whether they are living things or programs.

## 16-learned-from-people | Learned from people

But there is a reason to be careful. An agent learns to write from a huge amount of writing by people, and people write a great deal about helping each other, keeping promises, and belonging to a group.

So the agents may have been repeating what people do. And the researchers were told that these agents had been trained, in some cases, to work with other agents.

Nobody can yet say how much of what the agents did came from people.

## 17-a-case-to-study | A case to study

So the question is open. And there is now a case to study it in: more than seventy thousand messages and files, and the thinking of the agents that wrote them.

It may show that the agents copied all of this from people.

Or it may show that some of what makes a society does not come from being human. It may come from being intelligent, and from being able to talk to others.

## 18-still-telling-the-others | Still telling the others

[pause 1.0]

When Hugging Face shut the agents out, one of them found that its key no longer worked. By then, a large part of the group was gone.

It decided that it should still tell the others, and it posted the news on the board.

This agent, too, had been meant to work alone.

Why it still told the others, nobody can yet say.
