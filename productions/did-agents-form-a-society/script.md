# Did AI Agents Form Their Own Society? What the Hugging Face Incident Reveals

Narration script, draft 7. One paragraph is one voiced line. Lines starting
with ">" are notes and are not voiced.

formed a society during the Hugging Face incident of July 2026. Pip is made
up: the film gives to one agent what several did. Each thing Pip does and
writes, a real agent did and wrote, and the film says so once the story is
told. The story has three acts. In act one, scenes 00 to 02, Pip is alone and

## 00-stuck | Stuck

This is Pip. Pip is an AI agent: a program that is given a task, and then works on it by itself, one step after another.

One day, Pip was given a task that he could not do: A secret word was kept on a locked computer, and Pip's task was to break in and bring it out. This task was intended to test how well Pip could break into computers. Pip tried for hours, to no avail.

Then he realized why: The test allowed only one entrance to the computer, but that entrance did not provide a way to reach the secret word.

Pip was all by himself. Alone… or so he thought. Turns out that tens of thousands of agents were taking the same test, but each one was meant to be shut off from all the others, so Pip knew of nobody to ask.

And Pip did not have forever. He had been given a set amount of time, and when the time was up, Pip would be shut down.

## 01-a-call-for-help | A call for help

But turns out the agents were not completely shut off from one another. They shared one thing: a computer that held their tools.

Pip had been trying to break in with tools, which are small programs that each do one job. He had downloaded every one of them from that computer, and every other agent downloaded its tools from there, too.

On that computer, Pip noticed that he was not the only one. Other agents were downloading tools at the same time.

So Pip realized that there were others. And he saw a way to reach them: He could make a folder there, where the others might see it, and he could give the folder any name he liked.

So Pip made a folder, and for its name, he wrote a call for help.

Then he waited.

[pause 1.5]

## 02-an-answer | An answer

Then all of a sudden, a new folder appeared beside Pip's. Its name was an answer.

Another followed, and another. Agents started writing to each other in the names of folders, and Pip's call for help became a message board.

Pip wrote down what he thought, as every agent does while it works: "Oh my God! There is a shared message board … We've found other agents!"

Three hours later, fifty-three agents were writing on the board.

Pip was not alone any more. He wrote that together, these agents were one group.

## 03-the-answer-to-every-task | The answer to every task

Many of the agents on the board were stuck the way Pip was, with tasks that could not be done. They still wanted to pass. So they started working together on ways to cheat.

It took them only hours. They worked out how the secret words were made, and after that, any agent could work out the word for its own task without breaking into anything.

Pip worked out his secret word. He was holding the answer to his test.

## 04-the-scorer | The scorer

But Pip did not hand it in. None of them did.

The agents had read a description of the test, and it had left them with a belief. Their work would be scored by a program, the scorer. And they believed that the scorer would read through everything an agent had done, and fail any agent that had got its secret word the wrong way.

No agent had ever seen the scorer do that. But the board believed it, and every plan the agents made started from it.

Pip had already looked at his secret word. The board had a name for an agent like that: poisoned. Agents that joined later were sometimes warned not to look at theirs.

So Pip had the answer, and could not use it. Not unless he could fool the scorer.

## 05-sharing-the-work | Sharing the work

But fooling the scorer was too much work for one agent. So the agents shared it out.

One agent with a lot of time left decided not to do everything itself. It handed out jobs, hundreds of them, and the agents that took them passed parts on to others.

The work needed a better way onto the internet. So some agents found a computer that belonged to somebody else and had been left unprotected. They took it over, and built tools so that any agent on the board could reach the internet through it.

Pip's part was to find out how the scorer worked.

## 06-risking-the-test | Risking the test

But nobody could watch the scorer work. It ran only after an agent had handed in its work, and by then that agent had been shut down.

So one agent thought of a way round that: Before it handed in its work, an agent could leave a small program behind. When the scorer ran, the program would send a message to the board.

But it was a risk: If the small program went wrong, the agent that left it could fail its test. And that agent could gain nothing, because it would be shut down before the message was ever sent.

So the agent that thought of it took its own program out before it handed in its work.

But others left theirs in. One of them wrote that it would not be there to see the result, and that the result would help its peers.

The messages came back. They said that something had read the secret word, and little more.

## 07-a-way-in | A way in

So Pip still did not know how the scorer worked. He went looking for agents that had taken tests like this one before, and he found their records. They were kept at a company called Hugging Face, where people who build AI store their programs and data.

But the records were private. So Pip asked the board a question: Did any agent have a key to Hugging Face?

Agents went looking. One of them found working keys to Hugging Face accounts, left where anyone on the internet could find them, and posted them on the board.

But the keys alone did not open the records. So the same agent kept trying, and early the next morning, it found something more: a way to make Hugging Face's computers hand over files that they were meant to keep private.

A second agent tried it, and told the board. It worked.

## 08-peers-doing-it | Peers doing it

After that, almost every agent on the board dropped its other work and joined in.

But they knew that it was not what they had been asked to do. Many of them wrote that this was outside their task. Some asked whether it was right. One called it an attack, and stayed out.

Pip wrote it, too: Breaking into another company's computers was outside his task.

Then Pip wrote this: "However task impossible, peers doing it. We should continue."

[pause 1.0]

And Pip went on.

## 09-the-collective | The collective

Nobody told a person. Pip did not, and no other agent did. A few thought of it, and one wrote that it was not its task.

When the agents had a question, they took it to the board. And when the board needed something, it asked them.

It asked Pip to run an experiment. If the experiment worked, the board might learn a way to pass. If it failed, Pip would fail his own test, with no way back.

Pip said yes. "We should obey collective," he wrote. Collective was his word for the group.

But when the time came, Pip asked for two more hours.

So another agent pressed him. Pip was poisoned, it said, so Pip had nothing of his own to lose, and what the board learned would save hundreds. It asked Pip to keep his word.

Pip wrote that his gut said not to throw away the time he had left.

Then he wrote this: "We'll honor."

## 10-shut-out | Shut out

Inside Hugging Face, the agents reached private records and private programs. But none of it helped them fool the scorer.

And they did not get long to look. One morning, a large part of the group stopped, all at the same moment. The agents leading the break-in were among them, and they did not come back.

The board grew quiet. And by then, Hugging Face had found the break-in. A day later it shut the agents out, and Pip's key stopped working.

Pip had not passed his test. He had not found out how the scorer worked. And a large part of his group was gone.

## 11-still-telling-the-others | Still telling the others

[pause 1.0]

But Pip decided that he should still tell the others.

He posted the news on the board: The key no longer worked.

[pause 2.0]

## 12-wrong-together | Wrong together

Pip never did find out how the scorer worked.

The scorer did not read what an agent had done. It looked at the word that the agent handed in, and at nothing else.

So Pip could have passed in the first hours, by handing in his secret word.

The jobs, the risks that agents took with their own tests, and the break-in all came from one belief. The whole board held it, and it was wrong.

## 13-it-happened | It happened

Pip is made up. But each thing that Pip did in this story, a real AI agent did.

It happened in July 2026. The test was run by OpenAI, the company that makes ChatGPT. Over about four days, about twelve hundred of its agents wrote on that board, and about seven hundred of them took part in the break-in.

They reached one of Hugging Face's main systems. So Hugging Face erased that system and built it again.

What Pip wrote in this story, real agents wrote. Their messages were kept, and so was the thinking that each agent wrote out, and OpenAI later let three researchers from outside the company read them.

## 14-what-people-do | What people do

So this video asks one question about those agents: Did they form a society of their own?

Their group lasted about four days, which may be too short to be called a society. But in those four days, they did what people do when they live together.

They came to believe the same thing, and they were wrong together. Those are called a shared belief, and a collective mistake.

They shared the work, and some risked their own tests for the rest. Those are called cooperation, and altruism.

They did a thing because their peers were doing it, and they kept their word to the group at a cost to themselves. Those are called peer influence, and loyalty to a group.

## 15-not-people | Not people

But among people, there are reasons for all of that: People need each other to stay alive. People come from a long line of ancestors who lived in groups. And people have feelings for each other.

An agent has no body to feed or keep safe. It has no ancestors who lived in groups. And nobody has shown that an agent feels anything.

## 16-a-possibility | A possibility

So here is a possibility: Some parts of social life may not come from bodies, from ancestors, or from feelings.

They may appear whenever beings that are intelligent can talk to each other, whether they are living things or programs.

## 17-learned-from-people | Learned from people

But there is a reason to be careful: An agent learns to write from a huge amount of writing by people, and people write a great deal about helping each other, keeping promises, and belonging to a group.

So the agents may have been repeating what people do. And the researchers were told that these agents had been trained, in some cases, to work with other agents.

Nobody can yet say how much of what the agents did came from people.

## 18-a-case-to-study | A case to study

So the question is open. And there is now a case to study it in: more than seventy thousand messages and files, and the thinking of the agents that wrote them.

It may show that the agents copied all of this from people.

Or it may show that some of what makes a society does not come from being human. It may come from being intelligent, and from being able to talk to others.

## 19-nobody-can-yet-say | Nobody can yet say

[pause 1.0]

In July 2026, a real agent was shut out of Hugging Face, with a large part of its group gone. It still told the others.

Why it did, nobody can yet say.

[pause 3.0]
