# Every production tells a story

A production has to tell a story, hook the audience, and have what the user calls soul. It is the user's rule, and it holds for every production (see [The repo is a media factory](the-repo-is-a-media-factory.md)). A script that is accurate and in textbook order, with no people or stakes in it, is only passable.

The story does not need to be factual. The science behind it does. It is the user's rule, and it holds for every production: a production may follow a made-up character through made-up events, as the Gödel machines film follows Pip, while what it teaches about its subject is true and checked. Holding a story to what a source records, so that no one character can carry it, mistakes the rule.

The story is told, and not interrupted. It is the user's rule: a script that keeps stopping its story is boring. The story follows the standard three acts of storytelling: a setup, a confrontation, and a resolution. Nothing stops it at the start, and that includes the note on what is made up, which comes once the story is told. The narration has a storyteller's voice and not a classroom's: a line that sets up a lesson, as a line saying that today someone's task is a test does, is a fault. A story may be told in the past tense. An opening that presents the character and says what it is, as in "This is Pip. Pip is" and what an AI agent is, is fine, and a viewer must be told what the character is.

The story rises to its end. It is the user's judgement of a draft of the society film: a story whose highest point comes in act one, whose second act goes slack, and whose last act ends without a line that hits hard is wrong as a whole, however good its lines are. Claude's interpretation, not confirmed: the judgement holds for every production; the first act ends on a change that opens a larger question and leaves the character's want unmet; each scene of the second act raises what is done or what it costs; a limit set up in the first act, such as a time that runs out, comes back and is paid off; the last act holds the story's largest turn; and the last line of the production is short and concrete, and answers something from the opening. What follows the story, such as the note on what is made up and the production's argument, is brisk and builds to that last line.

The narration does not state as a fact what the story goes on to show is untrue. It is the user's point about the society film, where a line said that the agents were shut off from each other and that there was nobody to ask, and the next scene had them writing to each other. Such a line says what was meant to be, or what the character knew: the agents were meant to be shut off, and Pip knew of nobody to ask. Claude's interpretation, not confirmed: the point holds for every production's story.

The joins between scenes are tested with the rule that Trey Parker and Matt Stone use for story outlines: between any two beats the joining word is "but" or "therefore", and never "and then". A scene that can only be joined with "and then" is cut, merged, or given a cause. The user put this test forward; that it fits here is Claude's judgement. Claude's interpretation, not confirmed: the test holds between the beats inside a scene as well as between scenes, and it is run on the voiced lines, since a label in a script's list of joins does not make a join causal. Three things pass for a join and are an "and then": a join that only the narrator makes, such as a line that says where to start or what to look at next; a "but" that contradicts nothing before it; and a date or a "later" that opens a beat. In the Gödel machines film the joins are written out at the top of `script/script.md`.

**Why:** No further reason comes with the hook. Claude's interpretation: a correct explanation does not hold a general audience by itself; viewers need someone to follow, a question, and a payoff.

The hook is the opening itself, and the opening is a story, not a fact. The user's rule: a script whose opening has no hook is boring, however well it keeps the wording rules, and an opening that states a fact is not a story, however striking the fact and whatever quotation it carries.

**How to apply:** Give the viewer someone to follow, a question, and a payoff, and apply the same test to picture, music, and voice. Claude's interpretation of the opening, not confirmed: it introduces someone, puts them where they want something and are stopped, and tells what they did and what came of it in the order it happened, as the Gödel machines film does with Pip. It does not report an event from outside, open on a date and a count, or sum up what the production goes on to tell. The production's question comes out of that story, and is asked once the story has raised it. Past the opening, a scene ends on what happened or on someone's own words, never on a sentence that only defines a term. Claude's interpretation of telling the story without interruption, not confirmed: the scenes of the story carry no line whose job is to name a term, announce what to look for, cite a source, or put the production's question; the story shows a thing, and what it shows is named afterwards, in at most three things. In the three acts, the first gives someone a want and a world and ends when something they do changes that world; the second raises what the want costs until all seems lost; the third holds their last choice and what is true of the whole, and only then what the story shows about its subject. Claude's interpretation of the classroom voice, not confirmed: its marks are a line that announces the task as a lesson would, a rule stated as a rule, and a sentence in the middle of the story whose only job is to explain a word. After the line that presents the character, the story goes straight to the trouble, such as a line saying that one day Pip was given a task that he could not complete, and what else a viewer needs explained rides inside a sentence of the story. Test new or reordered scenes with the joining words. The hook comes from the story, not from hype words; see [State facts plainly](state-facts-plainly.md). Keep each scene to one idea; see [One idea at a time](one-idea-at-a-time.md). The wording rule is [Plain everyday words](plain-everyday-words.md), and the story stays literal; see [No analogies in script](no-analogies-in-script.md). What a production's story is about is settled for each production; for the Gödel machines film it is in [The Gödel film's subject and order](godel-film-subject-and-order.md). Claude's interpretation of the rule on made-up stories, not confirmed: the narration says what is made up once the story is told; and where a production's subject is a real event, what happened in it counts as the science, so a made-up character does and says only what someone in the event did and said, and the numbers, the dates, and the findings stay as the sources give them.
