# Every production tells a story

A production has to tell a story, hook the audience, and have what the user calls soul. It is the user's rule, and it holds for every production (see [The repo is a media factory](the-repo-is-a-media-factory.md)). A script that is accurate and in textbook order, with no people or stakes in it, is only passable.

The story does not need to be factual. The science behind it does. It is the user's rule, and it holds for every production: a production may follow a made-up character through made-up events, as the Gödel machines film follows Pip, while what it teaches about its subject is true and checked. Holding a story to what a source records, so that no one character can carry it, mistakes the rule.

The joins between scenes are tested with the rule that Trey Parker and Matt Stone use for story outlines: between any two beats the joining word is "but" or "therefore", and never "and then". A scene that can only be joined with "and then" is cut, merged, or given a cause. The user put this test forward; that it fits here is Claude's judgement. Claude's interpretation, not confirmed: the test holds between the beats inside a scene as well as between scenes, and it is run on the voiced lines, since a label in a script's list of joins does not make a join causal. Three things pass for a join and are an "and then": a join that only the narrator makes, such as a line that says where to start or what to look at next; a "but" that contradicts nothing before it; and a date or a "later" that opens a beat. In the Gödel machines film the joins are written out at the top of `script/script.md`.

**Why:** No further reason comes with the hook. Claude's interpretation: a correct explanation does not hold a general audience by itself; viewers need someone to follow, a question, and a payoff.

The hook is the opening itself, and the opening is a story, not a fact. The user's rule: a script whose opening has no hook is boring, however well it keeps the wording rules, and an opening that states a fact is not a story, however striking the fact and whatever quotation it carries.

**How to apply:** Give the viewer someone to follow, a question, and a payoff, and apply the same test to picture, music, and voice. Claude's interpretation of the opening, not confirmed: it introduces someone, puts them where they want something and are stopped, and tells what they did and what came of it in the order it happened, as the Gödel machines film does with Pip. It does not report an event from outside, open on a date and a count, or sum up what the production goes on to tell. The production's question comes out of that story, and is asked once the story has raised it. Past the opening, a scene ends on what happened or on someone's own words, never on a sentence that only defines a term. When a script argues from things to be found, it tells the viewer what to look for before the evidence, in at most three things, and the answer counts them off. Test new or reordered scenes with the joining words. The hook comes from the story, not from hype words; see [State facts plainly](state-facts-plainly.md). Keep each scene to one idea; see [One idea at a time](one-idea-at-a-time.md). The wording rule is [Plain everyday words](plain-everyday-words.md), and the story stays literal; see [No analogies in script](no-analogies-in-script.md). What a production's story is about is settled for each production; for the Gödel machines film it is in [The Gödel film's subject and order](godel-film-subject-and-order.md). Claude's interpretation of the rule on made-up stories, not confirmed: the narration says once what is made up, as the Gödel machines film does of Pip; and where a production's subject is a real event, what happened in it counts as the science, so a made-up character does and says only what someone in the event did and said, and the numbers, the dates, and the findings stay as the sources give them.
