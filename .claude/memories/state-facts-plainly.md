The narration states what happened in concrete terms, and does not tell the viewer how to feel about it. On 2026-11-14 the user read the opening of script draft 4 and objected to two things. The first line called the program's job "strange", and the user judged that word to be hype. The second line said the researchers pointed the program at "a problem of its own"; the user could not tell what that meant and asked for it to be made tangible.

**Why:** The user gave the two objections and no further reason. Claude's interpretation: the hook the user wants (see [The film tells a story](the-film-tells-a-story.md)) has to come from the facts themselves. A word that hypes an event makes the film sound as if it is selling something, and a phrase that points at an event without saying what it was leaves a general viewer behind.

**How to apply:** Leave out adjectives and asides whose only job is to make something sound remarkable (in draft 4 these were "strange", "unusual", "takes no chances", and a bare claim that the event really took place). Say who did what to what: name the fault, the task, the result. Replace idioms that stand in for an event (draft 4 had "ran into the same wall" and "went nowhere") with the event. In draft 5 Claude went through the whole script for both faults, not only the two lines the user pointed at; do the same on every later draft. The rule covers text on screen as well. It sits beside [Plain everyday words](plain-everyday-words.md) and [No analogies in script](no-analogies-in-script.md).
